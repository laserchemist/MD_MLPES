Refinement 4 · the idea

Why a vibrating, rotating molecule gives two branches

One molecule: the bond vibrates at ν₀ while the molecule turns at ω, so the light sees a beat: two lines, ν₀ − ω and ν₀ + ω.

Many molecules: at 300 K they turn at different rates; few barely turn, most turn near 12 cm⁻¹.

Add them up: a P branch and an R branch about 25 cm⁻¹ apart, with a dip at ν₀.

J. M. Smith · Temple University · 16 · schematic; the computed classical band follows on the next slide

For an undergraduate audience. The infrared light interacts with the molecule's dipole as seen in the laboratory. A vibrating bond makes the dipole oscillate at nu0; if the molecule also turns, that oscillating dipole points in a direction that rotates at rate omega, and the product cos(2 pi nu0 t) cos(omega t) equals half of cos[(nu0 + omega) t] plus half of cos[(nu0 - omega) t]: two frequencies, exactly like the sidebands of an AM radio signal. That is what each single trajectory on the previous slide shows (splitting equal to twice the rotation rate, r = 0.99). At 300 K the rotation rates follow a Boltzmann distribution (drawn for rotation about two axes, P(omega) proportional to omega exp(-omega^2 / 2 omega_T^2)); summing every molecule's pair of lines gives two smooth lobes, the P branch (lower wavenumber) and the R branch (higher), with few molecules contributing near nu0. In quantum mechanics the rotation is quantized and a third, sharp Q branch appears at the centre when the dipole change has a component along the rotation axis: classical dynamics cannot produce it, which is one reason for the VCI-plus-rotor band shapes later in the deck.
